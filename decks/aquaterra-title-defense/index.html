Our study sits in data science, specifically time-series forecasting, applied to marine municipal fisheries. It is analytics-first: we will use public secondary data and will not collect new field data. [→] It will cover the five MIMAROPA provinces and the 31 fish species in PSA's marine municipal fisheries tables, quarterly from 2002 Q1 to 2025 Q4 (PSA, 2026). 'Municipal' follows PSA's fisheries classification, and the province is our geographic level of analysis, so one record is one province, one fish category, one quarter. [→] Inputs: PSA landings and value, plus rainfall, sea surface temperature and typhoon tracks. Process: Random Forest will select the useful predictors, and a GAMM will forecast next-quarter landings, tested on quarters the model has not seen. Outputs: seasonal shortfall flags and their gross landing value at risk. Final coverage will depend on the completeness of the records.

Background of the Study

1 · Why it matters

Fisheries are key to food security and livelihoods.

FAO (2024)

2023 oil spill, Naujan, Oriental Mindoro: fishing banned, P400 million income lost.

Agaton et al. (2023)

Municipal fisheries here means catch from municipal waters (up to 15 km from shore), taken without a boat or with a boat of 3 gross tons or less.

2 · The decline
~45Mkg / year

National capture-fisheries trend: linked to overfishing and illegal fishing. Separately, MIMAROPA marine municipal production was 20,488.92 MT in Q1 2026, down 38.1% YoY.

Oceana (2026); PSA RSSO MIMAROPA (2026)
3 · Forecasting so far

A regional forecasting model for Philippine fisheries, with environmental data.

Bokingkito et al. (2026)

Our starting point.

The question  Can we see these drops coming, province by province?
When fish gets expensive at the palengke, it's usually because less fish came in. Fish isn't just food on our table; it supports jobs and income in coastal towns, and FAO points to fisheries as key to food security and livelihoods (FAO, 2024). [→] We've seen what happens when supply is disrupted. After the February 2023 oil spill off Naujan, Oriental Mindoro, fishing was banned; the fisherfolk interviewed lost their main income, some up to about US$1,300 a week, and relief did not cover basic needs (Agaton et al., 2023). [→] And the long-term trend is worrying: Oceana reports capture fisheries fell from about 2.6M mt in 2010 to 1.9M in 2023, roughly 45M kg lost every year, a national figure (Oceana, 2026). Oceana links the decline to governance gaps: overfishing, illegal fishing, commercial vessels entering municipal waters and weak enforcement. Separately, PSA RSSO MIMAROPA reported 20,488.92 MT in marine municipal fisheries production in Q3 2025, down 40.9% year over year; this is a MIMAROPA-specific statistic, not the national 45M kg/year figure (PSA RSSO MIMAROPA, 2025). [→] So can we see these drops coming? Bokingkito et al. (2026) built a regional forecasting model for Philippine fisheries using environmental data. That's our starting point. [→] So what's still missing?

III · Research Gaps

Research Gaps

Existing forecasts work at the regional level across the Philippines; AquaTerra will work at the province × fish species level within MIMAROPA. Bokingkito et al. (2026)
AquaTerra  turns forecasts into shortfalls and their value, province by province.
So here's what those studies already did. Bokingkito et al. (2026) developed fisheries forecasts at the regional level across the Philippines; AquaTerra will work at the province × fish species level within MIMAROPA. [→] Gap 1: there are no seasonal shortfall forecasts by province and fish category, the level where local offices plan. We will forecast each province and fish category and flag seasonal shortfalls. [→] Gap 2: forecasts give kilos, not value. We will attach a historical first-sale price to each shortfall, using PSA value and volume records (PSA, 2026). [→] Gap 3: shortfall flags and value rankings haven't been checked. We will test how accurate the flags are, and whether the rankings hold when prices change, on later quarters the model has not seen (Hewamalage et al., 2023). [→] Gap 4: typhoons, rainfall and sea temperature are often linked to lower catch (Noy et al., 2023; Holmes et al., 2021), but few studies check whether they improve a provincial forecast. We will test that, and report it if they don't help. This is prediction, not cause. [→] That's where AquaTerra fits: it fills the gap by turning forecasts into shortfalls and their value at province level. (Press R for the references behind each gap.)

IV · Research questions and objectives

Statement of the Problem and Specific Objectives

General objective  AquaTerra will forecast next-quarter municipal fish landings, flag seasonal shortfalls and estimate their gross landing value at risk for five MIMAROPA provinces.
Random Forest will rank and select the predictors. Nesslage et al. (2021)
GAMM will forecast landings with smooth curves. Holmes et al. (2021)
Initial research questions (preliminary)
Specific objectives
Our general objective: AquaTerra will forecast next-quarter municipal fish landings, flag seasonal shortfalls and estimate their gross landing value at risk for the five MIMAROPA provinces. Two algorithms, in one line each. Random Forest, many decision trees voting, will rank the candidate predictors and keep the useful ones (Nesslage et al., 2021). GAMM, a generalized additive mixed model, will add smooth curves for each selected predictor, plus province and fish-category effects, to forecast landings (Holmes et al., 2021). These are our initial research questions; we expect to refine them. Each is paired with exactly one specific objective. [→] SOP 1: What seasonal benchmarks will identify landing shortfalls for each MIMAROPA province and fish category, using PSA quarterly records from 2002 to 2025? SO 1: to build seasonal benchmarks for each province × fish category from PSA quarterly landings, 2002 Q1–2025 Q4. [→] SOP 2: How much will prior typhoon exposure, rainfall and sea surface temperature improve next-quarter landing forecasts from a Random Forest–GAMM workflow? SO 2: to develop the Random Forest–GAMM workflow and measure the contribution of selected predictors using reductions in Mean Absolute Error (MAE), comparing forecasts with and without them on held-out quarters. [→] SOP 3: What gross landing value at risk will the projected shortfalls carry when priced at historical first-sale prices? SO 3: to estimate the gross landing value at risk of each projected shortfall using historical first-sale prices, and rank the combinations. [→] SOP 4: How accurate will the forecasts be on held-out quarters (2022 Q2–2025 Q4), measured by MAE, RMSE and MASE against a seasonal-naive baseline? SO 4: to evaluate the forecasts on held-out quarters (2022 Q2–2025 Q4) with MAE, RMSE and MASE vs the seasonal-naive baseline, plus price-sensitivity and ranking-stability checks. The dashboard and the consultation with DTI or a fisheries agency are part of our scope, not separate objectives. (Click a row to highlight its pair.)

Scope. Our study area will be MIMAROPA: Occidental Mindoro, Oriental Mindoro, Marinduque, Romblon and Palawan, across the 31 named fish categories in PSA's marine municipal fisheries tables (PSA, 2026); PSA's 'Others' group will be left out. We will use public data from 2002 Q1 to 2025 Q4: PSA OpenSTAT, NASA IMERG, NOAA OISST and NOAA IBTrACS. Random Forest will select predictors and GAMM will forecast the next quarter; we will compare forecasts with seasonal benchmarks, value shortfalls with historical first-sale prices, evaluate on a 70/15/15 time split, show results on a web dashboard, and seek consultation with DTI or a relevant fisheries agency. [→] Limitations: selected provinces and marine municipal categories only; no commercial, aquaculture or inland fisheries and no field monitoring. Next quarter only; data gaps and delays may affect accuracy, and fishing effort and fuel costs will not be modeled. We will not establish causal effects of typhoons, rainfall or sea temperature. Gross landing value at risk will use historical prices, not income, profit or damage.

→ Next: Forecasting and Value at Risk
Our methodology has three parts: data preparation, forecasting and value at risk, then evaluation. This slide is data preparation. [Stage 1: Why these predictors?] We selected these environmental predictors based on studies published from 2021 onward. For mangrove extent and change, Nababa et al. (2026) documented a nonlinear relationship between mangrove extent and artisanal fish landings in Nigeria's Niger Delta, supporting its inclusion as a candidate predictor. For rainfall and sea surface temperature, Holmes et al. (2021) found that specific rainfall and temperature summaries improved forecasts of Indian oil sardine landings. For cyclone exposure, Noy et al. (2023) found fewer active fishing boats during and after tropical cyclones in the Philippines. These findings justify testing the predictors, while our study will assess whether they improve forecasts for MIMAROPA. The citations beside the environmental sources justify predictor selection; the dataset citations are retained in References. [Stage 1: Data sources] We will use five public source groups for the 2002 Q1 to 2025 Q4 study period: PSA OpenSTAT quarterly marine municipal landings and value for the five MIMAROPA provinces and 31 fish categories, with 23,250 total rows (PSA, 2026); NASA IMERG rainfall as daily or monthly time series (Huffman et al., 2023); NOAA OISST sea surface temperature as a daily time series (Huang et al., 2021); NOAA IBTrACS typhoon tracks at three-hour intervals (Gahtan et al., 2024); and Global Mangrove Watch (GMW) 4.1 with Landsat imagery, using annual GMW maps and 16-day Landsat time series (Bunting et al., 2026). The GMW dataset release is version 4.1.12: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21346457. [→ Stage 2] We will collect PSA landing volumes and compatible historical prices, then standardize names, units and quarterly dates, remove duplicates and annual totals, and log missing shares and consecutive gaps. Any category we exclude for insufficient coverage will be documented with the reason. Missing quarters will stay on the calendar; an unavailable value is not a zero landing. [→ Stage 3] Before filling any missing values, we will split the 96 quarters by time, about 70/15/15 after whole-quarter rounding: Train 2002 Q1 to 2018 Q3 (67 quarters), Tune or validation 2018 Q4 to 2022 Q1 (14), Test 2022 Q2 to 2025 Q4 (15). At each forecast date, preparation will use only information available by then, so later test quarters cannot leak into earlier estimates (Hewamalage et al., 2023). The ratio is our design choice. [→ Stage 4] We will compare SEADEC and structural time-series imputation with Kalman-based estimation, which performed well on marine landing series in Benavides et al. (2023); we will revalidate them on our quarterly data. KSSA will temporarily hide known blocks inside the training history, rebuild them and compare with the true values; we will pick the lowest-error method per series, flag every estimate, and leave long unsupported gaps unfilled. [→ Stage 5] Finally we will align everything to one row per province, fish category and quarter: rainfall as quarterly totals, SST as quarterly means, typhoons as counts of distinct qualifying storms. Prices will be matched by unit and kept for the valuation step only, never as a predictor. Table values are illustrative. Next: forecasting and value at risk.

Part two: forecasting and value at risk. The joined table from data preparation is the input. [Stage 1] Random Forest, an ensemble of decision trees, will learn only from the Train quarters and rank predictors by permutation importance: if shuffling a predictor raises the error, it matters. It will select predictors; it will not make the final forecast. Using Random Forest importance to guide a GAMM follows Nesslage et al. (2021). Bars are illustrative. [→ Stage 2] The Generalized Additive Mixed Model, or GAMM, will forecast next-quarter landings for each province and fish category, adding smooth curves for the season, lagged landings and each selected predictor, plus random effects for province and fish category (Holmes et al., 2021). Toggle 'Without' to see the version without weather terms. [→ Stage 3] Each forecast will be compared with the seasonal benchmark, the usual level for that province, category and quarter. If the forecast falls below it, the gap is flagged as a projected shortfall. Numbers are illustrative. [→ Stage 4] Shortfall tonnes times 1,000 times the historical first-sale price gives the gross landing value at risk in pesos. We will rank province × fish-category combinations by that value. It is gross value at stake, not profit or income. Values are illustrative. Next: how we will evaluate all of this.

Part three: evaluation. [1] We will lock the Test quarters, 2022 Q2 to 2025 Q4, 15 quarters, until the final check; Train and Tune quarters will be used only for fitting and selecting models (Hewamalage et al., 2023). [→ 2] Forecast accuracy will be measured with MAE, RMSE and MASE against a seasonal-naive baseline, which simply repeats the same quarter last year (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021). A MASE below 1 will mean our workflow beats that baseline. [→ 3] To answer SOP 2, we will run the forecasts with and without the typhoon, rainfall and SST predictors and measure the reduction in MAE on held-out quarters. If they don't help, we will report that. This is prediction, not cause. [→ 4] For value at risk, we will swap the historical first-sale price for a unit-value proxy and check whether the top-ranked province × fish-category combinations keep their places. 3 · How we will word results
✓ "Typhoon exposure improves forecast accuracy here."
✕ "Typhoons cause lower landings."

Same rule for rainfall, SST and any top-ranked predictor.

We will test whether environmental information improves forecast accuracy. We will compare a model with only past landings and seasonality against one with typhoon exposure, rainfall and sea temperature added, both evaluated on the held-out Test quarters. The gap: typhoons, rainfall and sea temperature are often linked to lower catch (Noy et al., 2023; Holmes et al., 2021), but few studies check whether they improve a provincial forecast. We will test that, and report it if they don't help. That is prediction, not cause. [→] Better accuracy will mean predictive usefulness only. Fishing effort, fuel prices, regulations and reporting changes can move together with typhoons and weather and also affect landings. Proving cause would need controlled or field studies, which are outside our scope. [→] So our results will say 'improves forecast accuracy', [→] never 'causes'.
